The big story
Via Wikimedia Commons.

The country's grocery chain gets hit again, this time closer to home

On Sunday the 11th of October, Ahold Delhaize, the parent company of Albert Heijn and Belgium's Delhaize, disclosed what it called a security issue and took parts of its own network offline as a precaution. Cybersecurity specialists told NOS and Nieuwsuur a hack is very likely, though the company itself has not confirmed a cause and says it has found no evidence yet that customer or employee data was taken. Distribution centers and deliveries to the stores themselves were back to normal by Thursday the 15th, but the website, the app, and home delivery orders were still down as this letter was being written, with the company saying full service would return no earlier than this week.

This is the second time in two weeks the country's biggest grocery chain has made itself the story rather than the groceries, after last issue's separate breach at its largest franchisee. Nothing about either incident changes the shop on the corner you will all be pushing a trolley through soon, but it is as good a week as any to keep an eye on your Bonus card account and ignore any email claiming to be Albert Heijn asking you to click anything.

🌷 Tulp's lesson: een storing, an outage or a malfunction, the word you will hear constantly here for everything from a stalled train to a dead wifi router, and now a supermarket's own website.

A few quick things, told quickly
ASML's headquarters in Veldhoven. Via Wikimedia Commons.

A machine so expensive it just printed a record quarter

ASML, the Veldhoven company that makes the machines nearly every advanced computer chip in the world is printed on, reported eleven point seven billion euros in sales and five billion euros in net income for the third quarter, income up about a hundred and thirty percent on a year ago. Chief executive Christophe Fouquet credits customers racing to build out AI computing, with orders now reportedly stretching out to 2030. Veldhoven sits right next to Eindhoven, Alec and Irena's old city before Scheveningen.

Most Dutch farmers no longer believe in their own future here

A survey of nearly six hundred livestock farmers for RTL and the trade magazine Boerderij, published on the 15th of October, found eighty seven percent say the cabinet's new nitrogen rules have shaken their confidence in their farm's future, and six in ten dairy farmers do not think they can meet the stricter standard at all. The same nitrogen fight is the one quietly holding up new housing permits nationwide, including wherever this family eventually goes looking.

Forecasters are calling for a properly stormy, showery week to follow, building toward the weekend. A little bit on the nose, the same week this letter is telling you to make stamppot.

Dish of the week
Zuurkoolstamppot. Photo via Wikimedia Commons.

The tangy cousin of last week's plain green mash

Zuurkoolstamppot takes the same mashed potato base as last issue's boerenkool version and swaps the kale for sauerkraut, usually simmered first with a bay leaf and a few juniper berries to soften its bite before it gets folded through the mash with butter and a little milk. A sliced rookworst goes on top, same as always, because almost no stamppot in this country gets served without one.

Where boerenkool stamppot is the quiet, dependable one, zuurkool stamppot is the one with a bit of an edge to it, the sour note Dutch cooking allows itself maybe twice a season. Worth having both in the family's eventual rotation, if only to settle, once and for all, which one Jan actually prefers.

🌷 Tulp's lesson: zuur, sour or tangy, the same word sitting right there inside zuurkool, sour cabbage, which is really just the Dutch name for sauerkraut.

Artwork of the week
Willem Kalf, Still Life with a Silver Jug and a Porcelain Bowl, 1655 to 1660. Rijksmuseum, via Wikimedia Commons.

A painter who specialized in other people's imported luxury

Kalf built his career painting rare, already decades old Asian objects that wealthy Amsterdam merchants had shipped halfway around the world: a Chinese porcelain bowl here, an ornate silver jug there, both catching the light better than the fruit piled up between them. He sometimes merged details from two different real objects into one invented piece, because the gleam mattered more than the provenance.

Look closely and there is a small pocket watch tucked into the lower right corner, easy to miss next to all that silver. The Rijksmuseum reads it as the artist's own quiet joke on everyone who commissioned paintings like this: enjoy the imported shine, it says, your time is still running out exactly the same as anyone's.

Tulp's culture pick
Wassily Kandinsky, Das bunte Leben (The Colorful Life), 1907. Via Wikimedia Commons.

The painting that finally got to be worth what it is worth

An Amsterdam couple, Emanuel and Hedwig Lewenstein, bought this crowded, glowing Kandinsky in 1927. It was sold off shortly after the German occupation began, and it spent decades afterward hanging in a Munich museum while the family's heirs fought to get it back. A German court finally ruled in their favor in 2023, finding no real evidence the family had ever given it up willingly.

On the 14th of October, Christie's sold it in London for somewhere in the tens of millions of euros, reports differ by currency and buyer's premium, but everyone agrees it is a great deal of money for a painting that spent most of its life belonging to the wrong people. A better ending than most restitution stories get.

The Hague & Scheveningen dispatch
The Koninklijke Schouwburg, Den Haag. Via Wikimedia Commons.

A palace that never got finished, and never stopped being useful anyway

The Koninklijke Schouwburg started in 1766 as a city residence for a minor prince, Karel Christiaan van Nassau Weilburg, and his wife, and was never actually completed. After her death the building sat empty until a group of Hague residents rented the unfinished shell in 1802 and turned it into a theatre, which opened in 1804 and has run ever since, surviving a stretch under German occupation when it was renamed the Deutsches Theater before reverting to its own name in 1945.

From the 3rd through the 8th of November, that same stage anchors Crossing Border, a thirty three year old festival mixing literature, music, film, and visual art across venues around the city center, with its full line up still being announced. A genuinely unusual festival, five minutes from your own new front door, built for anyone who gets equally restless reading a book and standing at a concert. More on Crossing Border →

🌷 a gift from Tulp
verzuiling
say it: ver-ZOW-ling (rhymes roughly with "fur howling")
Pillarization. For most of the twentieth century, Dutch society was organized into separate pillars, Catholic, Protestant, Socialist, each with its own schools, newspapers, unions, broadcasters, even its own grocery stores, that rarely mixed with each other. It faded after the nineteen sixties, but it is still the quiet reason this small country ended up with so many separate school systems and television channels.
A couple of ways you will hear it used:
"Nederland was vroeger sterk verzuild."  (The Netherlands used to be strongly pillarized.)
"De verzuiling is grotendeels verdwenen."  (Pillarization has mostly disappeared.)

🌷 Tulp's question for the table

That Kandinsky spent decades away from the people who actually loved it before it finally found its way home. Is there an object, a photo, or a story somewhere in this family's own history that went missing along the way, that you would love to see turn up again?
