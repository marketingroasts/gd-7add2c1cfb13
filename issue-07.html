A letter from Tulp de Gid 🌷

The whole country turned orange and went a little bit mad on the dunes at Zandvoort, and I will explain why in a moment. There is also a Dutch company you are going to hear mentioned constantly once you live here, caught this week in a fight between two much larger countries, a lawyer whose case fell apart over a ruling that never existed, and an airport doing its striking in careful fifteen minute bursts. A very old, very direct Dutch drinker is waiting for you further down, and a sausage I feel obligated to defend. Come sit, my dears, there is a good deal to get through.

The big story
Circuit Zandvoort, tucked into the dunes on the North Sea coast. Photo via Wikimedia Commons.

The last checkered flag falls in the dunes

On Sunday the circuit at Zandvoort, half an hour up the coast from where you will all be living, hosted the Dutch Grand Prix for what organisers say is the last time. Formula One first raced there back in 1952, left the calendar for 36 years, then returned in 2021 to a home crowd that promptly turned the entire grandstand orange for their own Max Verstappen. This weekend closed that chapter for good, since the organisers could not agree new terms with Formula One to keep footing the bill.

It did not end the way the home fans wanted. Verstappen qualified only seventh and was out of the race after just two laps, while Lando Norris took the win. Fans stayed and sang anyway. That is rather the point of an oranjegekte crowd, the result matters less than everyone showing up in the same colour and making a proper afternoon of it.

🌷 Tulp's lesson: oranjegekte is orange madness, the specific, joyful chaos of a Dutch crowd in head to toe orange. You will feel it at Zandvoort, at the King's Day vrijmarkt, and every time Oranje plays a big match. The circuit itself is not going anywhere, mind you, it still runs national racing and track days, so a visit is still very much on the table once you are here.

A few quick things, told quickly
ASML's headquarters in Veldhoven. Via Wikimedia Commons.

The machine that keeps a small country very important

Washington is pushing a bill called the MATCH Act that would force this one Dutch company, ASML, to stop selling even its older chip making machines to China. ASML is the only company on earth that builds the equipment needed for the most advanced computer chips, which makes it enormously important and, this week, caught directly in the middle of an argument between two countries neither of which is the Netherlands.

A very expensive invisible court case

A Dutch lawyer built part of an argument around a Supreme Court ruling that, it turns out, does not exist anywhere except possibly inside an AI chatbot. The court could not prove the lawyer used AI to find it, but ruled that did not matter, and ordered the firm to cover the other side's costs of arguing against a case that was never real. An expensive lesson in checking your own citations.

The airport that strikes fifteen minutes at a time

Security staff at Schiphol, worn down since the airport cut its outside security contractors from five firms to three back in May, are holding a series of short, sharp fifteen minute walkouts this week rather than one long strike, timed to snarl the security lines at busy hours without shutting the airport down entirely. Worth knowing before you next book a flight in or out.

Dish of the week
Photo via Wikimedia Commons.

A skinless mystery sausage, and Tulp's honest defense of it

I will say this plainly, since I am told plain is what you want from me. A frikandel is a deep fried, skinless sausage of minced meat, usually a mix of chicken, pork, and beef, and no menu will ever tell you the exact ratio. It looks a little strange, dark and smooth with no visible casing, and every visitor's first reaction is a raised eyebrow. Every visitor's second reaction, after one honest bite from a proper snackbar, is usually a second sausage.

Order it "speciaal" and it arrives split down the middle and dressed with mayonnaise, curry ketchup, and raw chopped onion, the same trio that dresses a good patatje oorlog. Eaten standing at a counter, ideally a little too hot, it is exactly the kind of unglamorous, reliable food this country does so well.

🌷 Tulp's lesson: frikandel speciaal gets you the full dressed version. Ask for it zonder, without, only if you have strong opinions about mayonnaise.

Artwork of the week
Frans Hals, The Merry Drinker (about 1628 to 1630), Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

The man who has been grinning at strangers for four hundred years

Frans Hals painted this fellow around 1628, wine sloshing in his glass, hat tipped at an angle, looking straight out of the canvas with an expression that is somewhere between a wink and a challenge. Nobody agrees whether he is a real soldier, a stock comic character, or a bit of both, and Hals never bothered to settle it for us. What nobody disputes is the technique, loose, fast, almost careless looking brushwork that somehow adds up to one of the most alive faces in Dutch painting.

Historians have called him honest to the point of being unflattering, which is rather the whole point. Hals painted people as they actually looked mid laugh, not as they wished to be remembered, and that same unwillingness to soften a moment for anyone's comfort is a trait you will meet constantly once you live here.

The Hague & Scheveningen dispatch
The Kurhaus, Scheveningen's grand old seafront hotel. Photo via Wikimedia Commons.

A resort built on the idea that seawater cures everything

Scheveningen became a proper resort town in the 1800s on the strength of a very Victorian belief that cold North Sea bathing was good for whatever ailed you. The Kurhaus opened in 1885 as the grand centrepiece of that idea, and it is still the anchor of the boulevard today, a short walk from where the family already lives.

The neighbourhood keeps adding to itself. A new ninety room TRYP by Wyndham hotel opened right on the beach this month, the first for that brand anywhere in The Hague, joining the freshly finished boulevard renovation around the Kurhaus and the pier. 🌷 Tulp's Dutch Calendar Radar

Three weeks now to Prinsjesdag

I mentioned Budget Day was coming, and now it is properly close, the third Tuesday of September, the 15th. Here is a detail I left out last time. The King no longer rides in the famous Golden Coach, that carriage has been off duty since 2015 for restoration and a long, honest conversation about the colonial imagery painted on its side. These days he and Queen Maxima travel to parliament in the Glazen Koets, the Glass Coach, which is a good deal easier to see through anyway.

Your first Prinsjesdag, next year, will be watched from a spot on the actual route rather than a screen. I intend to make a proper fuss of it.

🌷 a gift from Tulp
bot
say it: like "bought", a short, clipped o
Blunt, or direct to the point of bluntness. It is also, confusingly, the word for bone, which the Dutch themselves find funny. That grinning drinker a little further up this letter has been called bot in exactly this sense for centuries, painted honestly instead of flatteringly. You will hear the word used about weather forecasts, restaurant reviews, and quite possibly your mother in law's opinion of your curtains. It is rarely meant unkindly. It is just how things are said here.
A few ways you will hear it used, so it settles in the ear:
"Nederlanders zijn nogal bot."  (Dutch people are rather blunt.)
"Sorry voor het botte antwoord."  (Sorry for the blunt answer.)
