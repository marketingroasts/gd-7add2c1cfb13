The big story
The Binnenhof, the seat of Dutch government, in your future home city. Photo via Wikimedia Commons.

The government tears up its own budget, two weeks early

On Monday, after a weekend and most of Monday spent negotiating, the three coalition parties, D66, VVD, and CDA, agreed on next year's budget, and the headline is what they took back out of it. Roughly 6.5 billion euros in planned cuts to incapacity benefit, unemployment payments, and substitute pay for new mothers on maternity leave have all been scrapped. A separate plan to raise the retirement age faster, in step with life expectancy, saving another 2.8 billion euros, was cancelled too.

The right wing VVD had spent months insisting it would not move on those welfare cuts, so this counts as a genuine concession, not a rounding error. Part of the gap gets covered by roughly 750 million euros in higher taxes on the highest incomes, and the government is putting 1.5 billion euros toward softening rising energy prices for ordinary households. All of it becomes official on Prinsjesdag itself, two Tuesdays from now, read aloud in the Ridderzaal, the Hall of Knights, on the grounds pictured above.

🌷 Tulp's lesson: meevallen means to turn out better than feared, the opposite of tegenvallen. This whole budget reversal is a meevaller, literally "something that falls well." The Dutch keep a specific word on hand for a pleasant surprise, which tells you something about how often the alternative is the one expected.

A few quick things, told quickly
Dutch consumer fireworks. Via Wikimedia Commons.

The country that banned its own fireworks starts taking them back

With consumer fireworks now banned nationwide, today opens a six week amnesty: hand in whatever rockets or Cobras are still sitting in a cupboard somewhere, no questions and no fine, at any of thirty four drop off points a travelling van is visiting between now and the 9th of October. A survey found a third of households still have some left, and most of that third say they have no plan to turn them in, so this is one to watch rather than one that is solved.

The city that ran out of electricity to sell

Utrecht, the fastest growing city in the country, has frozen new electricity connections since the summer because its own grid is full. Homes and businesses waiting on a new or bigger connection simply go on a list now, with hospitals, drinking water, and housing given priority ahead of ordinary business expansion. The mayor says it could take years to fix. It is the clearest sign yet that the Dutch power grid itself, not permits and not money, is what actually limits how fast a Dutch city can grow.

The company quietly building the future of stroke care

Philips has been awarded nearly 34 million dollars in United States government funding to keep developing AI powered robots for stroke treatment, working alongside Johns Hopkins and Boston University. The goal is to let a surgeon operate a thrombectomy robot remotely, from something closer to a cockpit than an operating table, so a patient far from a specialist can still be treated in time. Philips is still headquartered in Eindhoven, and it is exactly this kind of unglamorous, extremely useful engineering the country you are moving to keeps quietly turning out.

Dish of the week
Bitterballen with mustard. Photo via Wikimedia Commons.

Bitterballen, the borrel's whole reason for existing

A bitterbal is a small deep fried ball of thick, well seasoned meat ragout, crisp on the outside and almost molten in the middle, and it exists for exactly one purpose: to be eaten standing up, with a toothpick, a napkin you will still need, and a cold beer in the other hand. It anchors what the Dutch call bittergarnituur, a whole plate of fried snacks built around it, and it is the single most ordered thing at a proper borrel anywhere in this country.

Making a good one at home takes patience rather than skill. A rich beef ragout gets made first, then chilled until firm enough to cut and roll, then breaded and only deep fried right at the end, so the outside sets crisp while the center stays soft enough to burn your tongue if you do not wait. Nobody waits.

🌷 Tulp's lesson: the mustard on the side is not optional. It is called borrelmosterd, and skipping it marks you as someone who has not been paying attention.

Artwork of the week
Judith Leyster, Self-Portrait (about 1630), National Gallery of Art, Washington. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

The painter who caught herself mid laugh, then vanished for two centuries

Judith Leyster painted this in about 1630, in her early twenties, and it is one of the liveliest self-portraits of the entire Dutch Golden Age. She has turned from her easel with a laugh already forming, brush and palette in hand, a violin player half finished on the canvas behind her, as though you had simply walked in on her working.

She ran her own successful painting workshop in Haarlem at a time when almost no women did, and was admitted to the painters' guild under her own name. She then largely disappeared from art history for two centuries, because later owners quietly reattributed her paintings to Frans Hals, that grinning drinker from a couple of issues back, or to her own husband, since a woman's signature simply sold for less. The museum only credited this very painting to her again in 1949.

The Hague & Scheveningen dispatch
The Scheveningen lighthouse, a short walk from where the family already lives. Photo via Wikimedia Commons.

A light that has been doing the same job for a hundred fifty years

The red and white striped lighthouse on the Scheveningen dunes first lit up on the 20th of December 1875, a cast iron tower built from nine segments stitched together, designed by the Dutch lighthouse engineer Quirinus Harder. It stands thirty meters tall with a hundred fifty nine steps inside, and the light itself used to rotate in a bath of mercury before it went electric in the 1960s. A hundred fifty years on, it is still a working aid to navigation, not just a landmark, which is rather the whole point of a good lighthouse.

The neighbourhood is marking the anniversary properly. Regular public tours run Wednesday and Saturday afternoons at 14:00, and a full evening celebration with guided light walks and performances is planned for the 13th of December. Closer at hand, Scheveningen's cultural venues are hosting a brand new two day festival called Aftersea on the 11th and 12th of September, music and film and art spilling across the seafront as the summer season properly ends. 🌷 Tulp's Dutch Calendar Radar

Two weeks now to Prinsjesdag, and a little suitcase you should know about

Prinsjesdag lands on the 15th of September this year, the third Tuesday of the month as always, and this time there is a genuine policy fight riding inside it, since the money in the big story above only becomes official once it is read into the record that day. Watch for a small, unglamorous detail: the Minister of Finance always arrives at the Ridderzaal carrying a plain leather briefcase, the koffertje, holding the actual Miljoenennota, the budget memorandum, whose name literally means "the note of millions." He holds it up for the cameras before going inside. It has been the same small ritual since 1946.

Your first Prinsjesdag, next year, you will get to watch that briefcase go by in person.

Culture pick
Rotterdam, host city for this year's Dutch Junior Songfestival. Photo via Wikimedia Commons.

A stage full of kids who are far braver than most adults

The Junior Songfestival, the Dutch national final that picks who represents the Netherlands at Junior Eurovision, lands in Rotterdam on the 19th of September, this year at the RTM Stage. It is the same format as the grown up Eurovision, glitter and key changes and all, except every performer is between nine and fourteen, which somehow makes the whole thing more sincere rather than less.

It is a genuinely good introduction to Dutch pop culture for a small person, unguarded and a little silly in exactly the way the adult version pretends not to be.

🌷 a gift from Tulp
borrel
say it: BOR-uhl, the r rolled a little
A casual gathering built around drinks and small fried snacks, bitterballen chief among them, with no fixed agenda beyond standing around together after work or on a Friday afternoon. It is a noun and it is also a verb, borrelen, to be doing exactly that. The Friday version has its own name, vrijdagmiddagborrel, and treating it as optional is a fast way to miss most of the actual office gossip.
A few ways you will hear it used, so it settles in the ear:
"Zullen we een borrel doen?"  (Shall we get a drink?)
"Ik kom zo, ben nog aan het borrelen."  (I will be there soon, I am still at drinks.)
