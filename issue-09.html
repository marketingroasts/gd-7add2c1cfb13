The big story
The Ridderzaal, where the King read today's speech, in the government quarter you will soon call your own. Photo via Wikimedia Commons.

The King spends his speech on something money cannot fix

Today was the real Prinsjesdag, the one I have been building up to for letters now. At half past one this afternoon King Willem-Alexander stood inside the Ridderzaal pictured above and read the Speech from the Throne while the House of Representatives, and half of Dutch television, watched. The women who work in that building marked the day the way they always do, in their very best hats, a small yearly parade of its own called the hoedjesparade.

What struck me was not the budget detail this year, most of which the government had already handed back two weeks ago in the story I told you last time. It was the King spending real weight on something no spending plan can write down. He called an aggressive Russia on Europe's eastern border a direct threat to Dutch security, not someone else's problem, and said democracy is not a simple calculation but a mentality that has to live in each of us. His word for what holds a divided country together was gemeenschapszin, community spirit, which he called the antidote to polarization. Not a bad thing to hear read aloud in a two hundred year old hall.

🌷 Tulp's lesson: hoedjesparade literally means "little hats parade." It has no official start time and no route, it is simply what people call the sight of the Binnenhof's own staff walking to work that one morning in their most elaborate hats, photographed every year without fail.

A few quick things, told quickly

The strike that happened anyway

Two days after the government announced it was reversing most of its welfare cuts, the country's rail, tram, bus, and ferry workers walked out anyway on the ninth of September, a full twenty four hours of nothing moving. The unions said their actual ultimatum had not been met. Most large employers had already told people to work from home that day, so economists called the real damage minor, but the message landed anyway: a concession offered two weeks before Prinsjesdag does not automatically buy back a union's trust.

Groundbreaking in the city you used to call home

ASML broke ground this month on a brand new campus outside Eindhoven, a second major site alongside its headquarters in nearby Veldhoven, built around what the company calls a "flow factory" model for assembling its enormous chipmaking machines. Once finished it is expected to employ twenty thousand people. Alec and Irena's old city keeps growing into one of the more important few square kilometers in the global economy, quietly, the way this country tends to do most things.

Five more places told to start building

The housing minister named five new locations, in Haarlem, Deventer, Weert, Leeuwarden, and Maastricht, that now carry an official label meant to fast track large scale construction, together aimed at roughly thirty thousand new homes by 2035. Maastricht alone is due about eight thousand of them. It will not fix a shortage this deep overnight, but it is one more sign the country is finally building at something closer to the volume the problem actually needs.

Dish of the week
Tompouce, in its usual bright pink. Photo via Wikimedia Commons.

A pink pastry with a very specific, very Dutch job

A tompouce is two layers of puff pastry sandwiching a thick slab of vanilla custard cream, the whole thing finished with fondant icing dyed a shade of pink the Dutch will defend to the death and everyone else compares to bubblegum. It sits behind the glass of every bakery in the country, roughly the size of your palm and entirely too easy to finish in four bites.

The bright pink version specifically comes out for a birthday, an anniversary, or the day you meet your in laws for the first time, though bakeries swap the icing to orange for King's Day and to other colors for other occasions. If it is sitting on the counter, something is being celebrated.

🌷 Tulp's lesson: the pastry is named for Napoleon's real, unusually short lieutenant General Thomas Thumb, though the older, more correct Dutch spelling is actually two words with no accent, tompoes. You will see both spellings and both are understood.

Artwork of the week
Gerard van Honthorst, Double Portrait of Frederik Hendrik and Amalia van Solms, Mauritshuis, The Hague. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

A prince who never expected to rule, painted twice at once

Gerard van Honthorst, working from a studio he opened right here in The Hague to stay close to the court, painted Frederik Hendrik alongside his wife Amalia van Solms in this double portrait, now hanging in the Mauritshuis a short walk from where Alec and Irena already live. Frederik Hendrik was the fourth son of William the Silent, never meant to inherit anything, until three brothers died without heirs and the job of stadtholder landed on him anyway in his forties.

He turned out to be good at it, expanding Dutch territory through the long war with Spain and using art and architecture, portraits exactly like this one among them, to make an improvised, elected princely house look as settled and legitimate as any hereditary crown in Europe. Four hundred years later his face is still doing that same quiet work of looking permanent.

The Hague & Scheveningen dispatch

That portrait's own house, as it happens, has been dark for a few weeks, which brings me to what is actually happening two minutes from Alec and Irena's door.

The Mauritshuis, closed for maintenance until the 20th of September. Photo via Wikimedia Commons.

The museum reopens, and the beach finally gets a real plan

The Mauritshuis has been closed since the 24th of August for maintenance and reopens to the public on the 20th of September, just in time to build toward something genuinely rare: from the 15th of October it swaps twenty five masterpieces with Madrid's Museo Thyssen-Bornemisza, a first ever exchange between the two collections, Van Eyck and El Greco included, built around love as its connecting theme.

Down on the coast, Scheveningen's long stalled Pier renovation finally has a real shape: a four star hotel, a smaller family one, a public park across the open deck, an expanded spa, and the wheel staying exactly where it is. Construction is now expected to start in early 2027, with the whole thing reopening in 2028, so by the time you are all settled it should be finished rather than still fenced off. 🌷 Tulp's Dutch Calendar Radar

Two and a half weeks to a city that celebrates surviving a siege

Circle the 3rd of October. Leidens Ontzet marks the day in 1574 the Spanish siege of Leiden finally broke, and the city has celebrated every year since with a very specific meal: hutspot, a mash of potato, carrot, and onion, said to have been found still warm in an abandoned enemy kettle the morning the siege lifted, plus herring and white bread handed out free at the town hall. A whole city built its founding legend around dinner.

We will get properly into it, hutspot and all, once the date arrives.

🌷 a gift from Tulp
troonrede
say it: TROHN-ray-duh
Literally "throne speech," the address the King reads every third Tuesday of September to open the parliamentary year. He does not write a word of it himself, the sitting cabinet does, so it is really the government speaking through him in its most old fashioned possible delivery method. This year's leaned unusually hard on democracy and community over the usual line by line preview.
A couple of ways you will hear it used:
"Heb je de troonrede gezien?"  (Did you see the speech from the throne?)
"De koning las de troonrede voor in de Ridderzaal."  (The King read the speech from the throne in the Ridderzaal.)

🌷 Tulp's question for the table

The King spent today's speech on gemeenschapszin, the sense of togetherness that holds a divided country steady, rather than on numbers. What is one small, ordinary gathering in your own week right now, a dinner, a call, a standing plan with a friend, that does that same job for you, and that you would want to keep once you are all finally in one place?

That is the letter, my dears. A King asking a divided country to hold onto its sense of togetherness, a transit strike that went ahead anyway, a chipmaking giant breaking ground in Alec and Irena's old city, thirty thousand new homes on the way, a pink pastry worth celebrating something for, and a prince who talked his way into looking permanent.

Give the call a good long stretch this Thursday. It is worth it.

Tot donderdag, which just means until Thursday, and which I am already looking forward to. Liefs, Tulp.
