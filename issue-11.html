We are counting down to 15 March 2027, little Amellia's third birthday, the day you all arrive for good. Twenty three weeks now, five months and a handful of days if you would rather count it that way.

A letter from Tulp de Gid 🌷

Lieve Geltovas, this week belongs to a city that has been waiting four hundred and fifty two years to stop being hungry, and I do not say that lightly. Leiden, a short train ride from your own future front door, throws itself the same party every single year on the third of October, and this time I am finally going to tell you the whole strange, wonderful story properly. Around it: a government that blinked first over six billion euros, a whole country that threw a street party for its own neighbours and broke a record doing it, a sculpture museum tucked into the Scheveningen dune, and a September that still, stubbornly, refuses to feel like autumn. Pour something warm and come sit with me.

The big story
Leidens Ontzet, the annual relief-of-the-siege festival in Leiden. Photo via Wikimedia Commons.

The night Leiden's hungriest year finally ended

In the autumn of 1574, Spanish troops had Leiden surrounded for months, and by the end the city was properly starving, eating leaves and shoe leather while the Spanish waited them out. Then, on the second of October, William of Orange's ragtag navy of Sea Beggars, flat bottomed boats that could sail over deliberately flooded farmland, finally broke through. The Spanish fled so fast in the night that they left their supper behind in the abandoned camp: a kettle of hutspot, still warm, a stew of whatever vegetables were on hand mashed together. Legend says a boy found it first. By morning of the third, the Sea Beggars were handing out herring and white bread to a city that had nearly forgotten what a full meal felt like.

Leiden has been throwing itself a party over it every year since, and this year's falls on Friday the second and Saturday the third of October, four days from when you read this. The city centre has already been closing streets and rerouting buses since yesterday, and from Friday noon to Sunday morning nobody is allowed to bring their own drink anywhere near the canals. The real schedule is glorious: a reveille at seven in the morning, free herring and white bread from half past, a remembrance service, choirs in the squares, a parade of floats, and fireworks over the water once it is properly dark. It is the whole story of this country in one weekend: something genuinely painful, four and a half centuries old, turned into the best party of the year.

🌷 Tulp's lesson: ontzet is the word for what happened, a siege being lifted, a relief. It is also, confusingly and rather beautifully, the modern Dutch word for being shocked or appalled. Context does all the work; a Dutch person is never confused which one you mean.

A few quick things, told quickly

The government blinks first, six billion euros' worth

After a summer of tense budget talks, the coalition has agreed to scrap its planned six billion euros in welfare cuts entirely. The two year cap on unemployment payments stays off the table, the long-term disability benefit stays untouched, and an earlier plan to raise the retirement age faster than life expectancy is also gone. It still has to survive weeks of parliamentary haggling, since the government has no majority of its own, but for now the safety net holds.

The whole country had a barbecue in the street

Last Saturday was BurenDag, National Neighbours' Day, and this year broke every record it has: over ten thousand four hundred registered street activities and roughly six hundred thousand people out doing them, the most in its twenty one year history. Coffee mornings, street barbecues, communal cleanups, the works. It says something rather nice about a country you are about to join, that an entire national holiday exists just for meeting the people next door.

Autumn keeps missing its cue

It is the last week of September and the thermometer is still climbing toward twenty nine degrees in places, warm enough that Kijkduin, practically next door to Scheveningen, is still drawing beach crowds who by rights should be back in coats. Spare a thought for the reverse of it: while the Dutch stretch out one more summer weekend, I am told Portland has finally, properly, tipped into real autumn. Dish of the week
Hutspot met stooflap. Photo via Wikimedia Commons.

The dish an empty enemy camp left behind

You already met the legend in the big story above: potatoes, carrots and onions, boiled together and mashed into one honest orange heap, supposedly found still warm in the Spanish camp on the morning Leiden was finally relieved. Whether or not that exact kettle ever existed, hutspot has been Leiden's dish ever since, and on the evening of the second of October the city still sits down together for a communal hutspot dinner before the real festivities begin. Traditionally it comes with a slice of slow-braised beef, klapstuk, laid on top with the gravy poured into a little well pressed into the mash.

It is simple, cheap, and exactly the kind of food that makes sense after months of having almost nothing: no fuss, entirely filling, and better the day after. Amellia will find it exactly as easy to eat as any mashed potato, which is rather the point.

🌷 Tulp's lesson: hutspot comes from hutsen, an old word for shaking or tossing things together, which is exactly what the dish is: whatever you have, shaken into one pot.

Artwork of the week
The reception of William of Orange and the Sea Beggars by mayor Van der Werf and the Leiden city council. Via Wikimedia Commons.

The painting that came home

This large history painting, close to two metres across, shows the moment on the fourth of October 1574, the day after the relief itself, when William of Orange arrived in Leiden and was formally received by the city's mayor, Pieter Adriaanszoon van der Werf, the man who had held the starving city together through the worst of the siege. It is a proper piece of civic pride, painted to be looked at by a whole town that had earned the right to celebrate.

Here is the part I love best for you: for decades this painting hung quietly in a castle near Antwerp, in the private collection of a Flemish family, entirely out of sight. It only found its way back to Leiden recently, through an auction house, more than four centuries after it was painted. A piece of the city's own story, wandering for generations before it finally came home, rather like this family of yours is about to do in reverse.

🌷 Tulp's Dutch Calendar Radar

One day for the humans, the next for the pets

Right on the heels of all that Leiden excitement comes a much smaller, gentler date: Dierendag, Animal Day, on the fourth of October. It is not a day off or a big public event, just a small national soft spot, where pets get an extra treat and a bit of extra fuss, and a fair few schools and shelters mark it quietly. After a weekend of sieges and fireworks, I rather like that the calendar's next stop is just, be a little kinder to the dog.

The Hague & Scheveningen dispatch
Museum Beelden aan Zee, built directly into the Scheveningen dune. Via Wikimedia Commons.

A museum you could walk straight past, and a show worth stopping for

Museum Beelden aan Zee sits right on your own stretch of coast, and it is easy to miss entirely: the architect Wim Quist built it into the dune itself, sand coloured and low, so it almost disappears into the landscape rather than announcing itself. It is the only museum in the country devoted purely to sculpture, and it has been quietly one of Scheveningen's best kept secrets since 1994.

Right now, through the first of November, it is showing Birthland, a solo exhibition by Femmy Otten, this year's winner of the Per Abramsen Sculpture Prize. Her sculptures and drawings turn motherhood and care into something worth looking at closely, which feels like exactly the right show to have on the books before a two year old and her whole extended family end up living five minutes from the door. 🌷 a gift from Tulp
ontzet
say it: ont-ZET
The word behind this whole letter: a siege lifted, a rescue, a relief. Modern Dutch also uses it for being shocked or appalled, which sounds like a contradiction until you remember both meanings are really about the same thing, the ground shifting suddenly under your feet.
A couple of ways you will hear it used:
"Leiden werd ontzet op 3 oktober 1574."  (Leiden was relieved on the 3rd of October 1574.)
"Ik was ontzet toen ik het hoorde."  (I was appalled when I heard it.)

🌷 Tulp's question for the table

When you picture the moment you are all finally together for good, forget the big special day itself. What is the first completely ordinary thing, an ordinary Tuesday sort of thing, you most want to do together?

That is the letter, my dears. A four hundred and fifty two year old siege finally breaking, a kettle of stew that fed a starving city, six billion euros that stayed in people's pockets, a whole country out meeting its neighbours, and a museum tucked so quietly into a dune you could miss it walking straight past.

Enjoy your call this Thursday, and if anyone happens to be near Leiden this weekend, go and stand in the noise of it at least once.

Tot snel, which just means see you soon. Liefs, Tulp.
