A letter from Tulp de Gid 🌷

Lieve Geltovas, this week the country learned a few things about trust, the slow kind and the sudden kind both. The supermarket everyone in this country shops at had a very bad week with its own data, someone deliberately lit a fire under the train line you will use constantly, Groningen's museums quietly declared war on a new kind of camera, and a Nobel Peace Prize landed about five minutes from where you will actually be living. The economy, for once, also had some genuinely decent news to report. Put the kale on to boil. This is a plain, grounded sort of letter, which feels about right for the week it is describing.

The supermarket everyone trusts just lost a lot of trust

Bun, the single largest Albert Heijn franchisee in the country with twenty nine supermarkets and about three thousand five hundred staff, has been hit by a ransomware attack. The attackers took passport copies and personnel files, salary details, sick leave records, performance reviews, reaching back to at least 2017 and covering current staff and former staff alike. Bun brought in a specialist investigation team and told affected employees on the 10th of October. The attackers are now pressing the company to pay, with conflicting claims about how much of the stolen data has already been published.

Albert Heijn is not an abstraction here. It is the shop on the corner, the one this family will be pushing a trolley through every week once you are all living five minutes from Alec and Irena. Nothing about this changes that, but it is a fair nudge toward the same ordinary caution anywhere: think twice about which documents go into a Dutch employer's or landlord's paperwork, and ask what happens to them afterward.

🌷 Tulp's lesson: persoonsgegevens, personal data, the phrase you will see on every Dutch privacy consent form you are asked to sign, from a gym membership to a daycare registration.

The economy had a rare good news day

The IMF now expects the Dutch economy to grow by 1.5 percent this year, up from the 1 percent it predicted back in July, even as it warned that high prices will keep squeezing households. The flip side arrived the same week: CBS confirmed inflation rose to 3.4 percent in September, pushed up mostly by fuel and energy costs. Growing faster, and still not cheap. Very on brand.

Someone deliberately lit a fire under the train schedule

At half past two in the morning on Saturday the 10th of October, someone forced open a technical cabin at a rail bridge between Rotterdam and Delft and set a fire inside it. Thirty five cables were damaged, nobody was hurt, and trains between Rotterdam Centraal and Delft Campus stayed suspended into Monday, with replacement buses running in between. Investigators are calling it sabotage. Worth filing away, since this exact stretch of track is the one you will all ride constantly once you are living in the Scheveningen and Hague area.

Groningen's museums just banned a certain kind of camera

Thirty five cultural organisations across Groningen, museums, clubs, festivals, the lot, have jointly written a ban on AI smart glasses into their house rules, worried the devices let people film and analyze strangers without anyone's knowledge or consent. They are now asking the national government for actual regulation and calling on hospitality, sport, and healthcare to follow their lead. The Dutch privacy regulator already agrees that sharing images from camera glasses without everyone's permission breaks the law.

Dish of the week
Boerenkool stamppot. Photo via Wikimedia Commons.

The mash that proves Dutch comfort food has exactly one rule: keep it plain

Boerenkool stamppot is kale and potato boiled soft and mashed together into one dense green pile, loosened with a little butter and milk, with a sliced smoked sausage, rookworst, laid on top or stirred straight through. Nobody dresses this dish up. Most people press a small well into the middle of their plateful, a kuiltje, just deep enough to hold a pool of gravy, and that small act of care is about as decorative as the whole meal gets.

It is the most traditional of all the stamppots for a reason: two vegetables, a starch, a sausage, nothing performed about it, built by a culture that has never trusted a dish for trying too hard to impress anyone. It keeps for days and reheats better than it started.

🌷 Tulp's lesson: kuiltje, little well or little pit, from kuil with the diminutive ending the Dutch attach to almost anything they are fond of. A whole national comfort food built around a word that just means "small hole."

A lantern walk is coming, four Thursdays from now

Nothing is quite on top of us this particular week, so here is one further out on the horizon: Sint Maarten falls on the 11th of November, when children all over the country walk the streets after dark with homemade paper lanterns, singing at each door for a sweet in return. No costumes, no big production, just children, candlelight, and a lot of slightly off key singing. Four Thursdays from this one.

Artwork of the week
Jacob van Ruisdael, The Windmill at Wijk bij Duurstede, c. 1670. Rijksmuseum, via Wikimedia Commons.

The windmill that out-dramas every church and castle around it

Ruisdael paints the small town of Wijk bij Duurstede, its castle and its church included, and then quietly lets a working windmill upstage all of it, planting the mill on the highest ground and giving it the whole left side of an enormous, churning sky. The River Lek runs flat and pale across the foreground, exactly the kind of big weather this country specialises in.

It is a very Dutch kind of hierarchy. The castle stands for old money, the church for old authority, and the painter hands the starring role to the machine that was actually doing useful work that day. Worth remembering before a certain toddler's first trip to Kinderdijk.

The Hague & Scheveningen dispatch
The Vredespaleis, the Peace Palace, seen through its own front gate. Via Wikimedia Commons.

A Nobel Prize just landed about five minutes from your new front door

The Peace Palace opened in 1913, built with money from the American steel magnate Andrew Carnegie specifically to give the world's disputes somewhere to be settled with lawyers instead of armies. It still houses the International Court of Justice and the Permanent Court of Arbitration, which is the whole reason The Hague gets to call itself a City of Peace and Justice rather than just the seat of government.

That identity had a genuinely good week. Navi Pillay, who sat as a judge at the International Criminal Court here in The Hague from 2003 to 2008 and later chaired the United Nations inquiry into the war in Gaza, was awarded the 2026 Nobel Peace Prize on the 9th of October for her work promoting peace and international law. A reminder, right as you are about to move here, that this particular small city keeps producing people the rest of the world eventually has to listen to. More on Navi Pillay's Nobel Peace Prize →

🌷 a gift from Tulp
nuchter
say it: NUCH-ter (that middle sound is the same throat-clear as in "Scheveningen")
Level headed, sober, unimpressed by flash. The Dutch consider it a compliment of the highest order, and a whole lot of this week, the plain mash, the plain windmill, the plain economic update, was nuchter without anyone having to say so.
A couple of ways you will hear it used:
"Blijf nuchter."  (Stay level headed, keep your feet on the ground.)
"Hij is een nuchtere jongen."  (He is a down to earth guy.)
